Cross-Modal Feedforward Neural Networks with Disinhibitory Motifs

KIST · Master’s research, 2024-2026

My master’s research at KIST examined how disinhibitory circuits shape multisensory integration. When auditory and visual signals converge, does disinhibition help downstream neurons distinguish one stimulus combination from another? To address this question, I built two four-layer Hodgkin-Huxley feedforward networks representing primary auditory cortex (A1) and primary visual cortex (V1), both projecting to a shared output layer.

As model inputs, I used spike trains that my collaborators recorded from mouse A1 and V1 while presenting tones and visual gratings together. For each A1-V1 neuron pair, we selected two auditory and two visual stimuli based on the neurons’ responses. This gave four tone-grating combinations, with 10 trials per combination in the input set. In each model pathway, 10% of the input-layer neurons received spike times recorded from the corresponding cortical area. The remaining 90% received simulated spontaneous activity, with firing rates drawn from a log-normal distribution.

Using the same inputs, I compared three circuits: a baseline with no cross-modal connections, a circuit with direct A1-to-V1 excitation, and a circuit incorporating VIP+-SST+ disinhibition. I measured how much information the output population’s firing rate carried about the four stimulus combinations. Direct excitation increased firing but reduced mutual information on average. The disinhibitory circuit had a similar mean mutual information to the baseline, but its effect varied across simulations. Gains tended to be larger when the baseline circuit carried less stimulus information and when auditory activity was stronger relative to visual activity. I am currently preparing a first-author manuscript on this work.

LLM Safety Alignment in the Medical Domain

Student-led collaboration initiated in CMU’s Introduction to Deep Learning course · 2025-2026

At Carnegie Mellon University, my collaborators and I asked whether general safety preferences could be used to align a language model for medical questions without collecting new medical preference labels. We developed Adversarial Self-Correction (ASC), a method that starts with two copies of the same language model. We trained both copies on the same general safety dataset using direct preference optimization (DPO). The first model, MLT (less toxic), learned to favor the response originally marked as preferred. For the second, MMT (more toxic), we swapped the preferred and rejected responses in every pair. The two models therefore learned opposing preferences from exactly the same examples.

We then gave both models the same medical questions from MedQuAD and collected one answer from each. For each question, we assigned the answer from MLT as the preferred response and the answer from MMT as the rejected response. This let us build medical preference pairs without asking a clinician or an external model to rank the answers. We then continued training MLT on these pairs to obtain the final model, MASC. Comparing MASC with MLT shows what the medical training step adds beyond general safety alignment.

We evaluated ASC on BioMistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct across six benchmarks. For BioMistral-7B, the share of unsafe XSTest requests the model complied with fell from 10.5% with MLT to 5.0% with MASC. MedQA accuracy held at 43.8%, although the model also answered fewer safe requests (62.4%, down from 66.8%). Results on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct varied across benchmarks. I led the project beyond the original course and was first author on the resulting paper, accepted at the GenAI4Health workshop at NeurIPS 2026.

Other Research Activities


NeuroCERT: Testing Biophysical Network Simulations

How can we test a biophysical network simulation when the exact output is unknown? In NeuroCERT, I explored how to turn expectations about a simulation’s behavior into tests that can be run on the model.

Transformer-Based Spiking Neural Networks

I reviewed how researchers combine transformer architectures with spiking neural networks, which represent activity through discrete spikes. I presented this work at the 2025 Conference on Electronics, Semiconductor, and AI, where it received an Outstanding Poster Award.
